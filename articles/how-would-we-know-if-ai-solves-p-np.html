How Do We Know If We Have Solved P = NP?

The machine can flood the room with claims. The hard question is who can tell which claim is real.

By Emily Hsiang · Public review opened 2026-07-18 · Long-form essay

If P = NP were solved in the conventional sense, we would expect a certain kind of announcement.

An announcement with institutional weight behind it: a serious preprint, known mathematicians reading it, objections answered, maybe a formalization effort, eventually journals, seminars, and a field slowly agreeing that the proof holds. The claim would become news only after a verification process had already begun to make it knowledge.

But AI creates a stranger and more uncomfortable situation.

A high school student, working independently, posts on LinkedIn that ChatGPT helped them find the missing reduction and that, working together, they have solved P = NP. Separately, a college sophomore, also working independently, uploads a 142-page proof to Reddit and declares that, working with Claude, they have solved the long-standing problem. A third independent claimant shares a GitHub repository with diagrams, lemmas, and a claimed Lean formalization checked with Gemini, presenting it as their own solution to one of theoretical computer science's most critical open questions. Another person, working independently again, builds a website declaring that, working with ChatGPT, Kimi, and DeepSeek, she has solved P = NP. In a fifth independent effort, a YouTuber publishes a one-hour series explaining a breakthrough they say they produced independently with a combination of multiple open-weight AI models, including DeepSeek, Qwen, and Inkling.

That is the situation: many independent claims of solving one of the most important questions in the history of theoretical computer science, arriving through ordinary public channels before any shared verification process has made them knowledge.

When proof-shaped claims become cheap and easy to generate, who gets to make the call?

The myth in the room

Here is the misconception, the one our culture keeps smuggling into every conversation about AI and genius:

A regular person mostly needs access to one original idea. Once the idea appears, the world can take care of the rest.

You can hear it in founder mythology. You can hear it in the way breakthroughs get retold as lightning strikes. You can hear it in the advice given to young people: do not get trapped in expertise, expertise makes you conventional, just think differently.

The myth is emotionally convenient because it makes greatness look like a door that might open for anyone at any moment. The historical record is less convenient. Shakespeare had a trade. Darwin had barnacles. Picasso had academic draftsmanship before he shattered the figure. Bach copied scores by hand. Karikó stayed with mRNA for decades before the world caught up. None of these stories begin with a blank mind receiving a perfect bolt from the sky.

The pattern is not that deep work guarantees greatness. It does not. The pattern is that field-changing work almost never appears before long contact with a field's materials, constraints, failures, and standards. 4 5 6 56 58

But the myth survives because it has a second source of power: it confuses production with recognition. It asks, can a new thing be made? That is not the hardest question anymore.

The harder question is: if a new thing is made, who can tell whether it matters?

The question that breaks it

Return to the old monkey-and-typewriter thought experiment. Enough monkeys, enough typewriters, enough time, and eventually one of them types Hamlet.

Fine. Suppose it happens.

There is a room full of paper. Most of it is garbage. One stack contains Hamlet, perfect from the first line to the last. The monkey does not know. The typewriter does not know. The paper does not glow. The masterpiece has no built-in alarm.

Now someone has to walk into the room and read.

That reader needs English. More than English, they need a sense of drama, blank verse, genre, influence, compression, and what had already been tried. Without that background, Hamlet is just one more stack in the pile. The masterpiece exists physically, but not culturally. It becomes valuable only when a field can recognize it.

That is the missing half of creativity. The standard definition requires both originality and effectiveness; Csikszentmihalyi adds the social mechanism: a creative act becomes creative when a field recognizes a valuable change to a domain. 1 3

Originality is the monkey's part. Recognition is the reader's part.

AI changes the scale of the room. It does not abolish the reader.

Cut to the reader

Put two people in front of a chessboard for five seconds. One is a beginner. One is a master. Then cover the board and ask them to rebuild the position from memory.

If the position came from a real game, the master can often reconstruct it with startling accuracy. The beginner cannot. But now do the strange part: scatter the same pieces randomly, show the board again, and the master's advantage largely disappears. Same eyes. Same five seconds. Same thirty-two pieces. Suddenly the expert looks much more ordinary. 11

What did the master see in the real position that disappeared in the random one?

That is the reader problem. An expert is not simply a person with more facts stored somewhere in memory. An expert is a person whose training has changed the unit of perception. Where the beginner sees pieces, the master sees a threat, a pawn structure, a familiar weakness, a shape with a history.

Chess research gives the mechanism. Masters remember real board positions far better than novices because they do not see thirty-two isolated pieces. They see structures. Ericsson and Kintsch later described this as long-term working memory: experts use stored structures to make a tiny working-memory window behave much larger. 11 12

The novice and the expert may both combine four things in mind. The difference is that the novice's four things are small. The expert's four things are whole compressed worlds.

The same thing happens in the body. Expert dancers watching movements from their own trained style show stronger action-observation responses than when watching unfamiliar movement. Same video, different perception. Training changes what the scene contains. 14

A mathematician sees a proof step straining under too much weight. A chemist sees a molecule that looks elegant and will fail at synthesis. A software architect sees a beautiful demo that will become unmaintainable six months from now. The expert is not merely thinking more thoughts. They are seeing a different object.

Economists Cohen and Levinthal gave this capacity a name: absorptive capacity, the ability to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it. The cruel part is that absorptive capacity is built from prior related knowledge. You cannot outsource the recognition of value to a person who has not learned what value looks like. 18

Information has no value to an agent with no capacity to absorb it.

That sentence is the key to the whole room.

The apprenticeship montage

Go back to molecular biology in the late 1970s, before PCR, and the room feels slower.

DNA is no longer mysterious. The double helix has been drawn on blackboards for decades. Sequencing exists. Restriction enzymes exist. Gels, probes, plasmids, bacterial colonies, radioactive film: all the furniture of the new biology is already there. But the thing a researcher wants is often absurdly small. A single gene. A tiny mutation. One stretch of sequence buried inside a human genome. A few molecules in a drop of blood. The code is there, but it is faint.

The naive dream is visual: build a better microscope, look harder, somehow see the letters directly. But the useful reality is messier and more chemical. You cut DNA into fragments. You separate them through a gel. You transfer them to a membrane. You wash them with a labeled probe and wait for a dark band to appear on film. Or you put a fragment into a plasmid, put the plasmid into bacteria, grow colonies, screen them, and hope the colony you need is in the plate. The work is not one clean act of seeing. It is a chain of amplification, separation, tagging, waiting, and interpretation.

So the field had several natural directions. Better instruments. Better probes. Better sequencing chemistry. Better cloning. Better ways to make bacteria carry the fragment long enough for the laboratory to catch it. All of them were rational, and all of them treated the same bottleneck as labor: if the target is too small, build a longer route around it.

The key moment is the early 1980s, especially 1983, at Cetus Corporation in Emeryville, California. The method would be presented to the field in 1985 and later win Mullis the 1993 Nobel Prize, but at first it was just an idea inside a lab notebook and a company culture full of DNA synthesis work. Mullis saw a more violent simplification. Do not only search for the DNA. Do not first persuade bacteria to carry it. Make the chosen segment copy itself in a tube. Heat separates the two strands. Short primers mark the boundaries. DNA polymerase extends from those primers. Repeat the cycle, and the copies double, then double again, then double again. Later thermostable polymerases and automated thermal cyclers made the method practical at scale, but the conceptual turn was already there: turn a nearly invisible target into millions of copies. 70

Why did that solution become visible to Mullis?

Start before Cetus. Mullis earned his PhD at Berkeley in 1972, but his route into molecular biology was crooked rather than ceremonial. In his Nobel lecture, he describes taking astrophysics courses, publishing an early paper far from DNA chemistry, doing medical research, returning to Berkeley, working at UCSF, and only later becoming fascinated by DNA synthesis after hearing about the chemical synthesis and cloning of a gene. He began studying DNA synthesis in the library and looking for work making DNA molecules. 70

Cetus hired Mullis in 1979, and by then he was not watching the DNA revolution from a distance. He was inside a biotechnology company where synthetic DNA was becoming a daily material. Within a couple of years, his lab had a machine turning out short DNA sequences faster than the company's molecular biologists could use them. Primers were not a word in a textbook. They were things he could order, design, move through a queue, and hold in the workflow of the lab. 70

The lab next door was working on detecting point mutations. That sounds narrow, but narrow problems are where deep people learn the shape of reality. A point mutation is almost nothing: one letter changed inside a vast biological document. If the target sat on a purified plasmid, the chemistry could behave. If the target was a single-copy sequence inside human DNA, the signal disappeared into the noise. Mullis was living inside that mismatch: the tools worked when the target was concentrated and failed when the target was rare.

Then the pieces began to rearrange. If one short DNA primer could bind near a target, why not two, one on each strand? If polymerase could extend from a primer once, why not let it extend, heat the strands apart, cool them, and let the primers bind again? If the second round used the first round's products as templates, then the process would not just add copies. It would multiply them.

Notice how unromantic the ingredients are. Heat. Cool. Primer. Polymerase. Repeat. Nothing in the list looks like genius from the outside. The genius is the compression: years of contact with DNA synthesis, mutation detection, plasmids, gels, enzymes, failed specificity, and lab automation collapsed into one mental object. Mullis could see PCR because the problem was not simply we need more DNA. To him, it could be broken into moves: separate strands, mark boundaries, extend a copy, repeat, automate, protect the enzyme from heat.

Depth did not give Mullis one extra fact. It gave him a different decomposition of the problem, and therefore a different set of possible moves.

The same hidden apprenticeship is easier to miss in famous lives. Shakespeare in grammar school, Latin rhetoric drilled until the moves become reflex. Shakespeare in the London theatre, learning what keeps a crowd breathing and what loses it. Picasso drawing like an academic master before Cubism. Darwin spending years on barnacles. Wallace collecting specimens across the Amazon and the Malay Archipelago. Faraday binding scientific books, then reading them, then finding his way into the laboratory. Ramanujan filling notebooks in isolation, then needing Hardy to understand what had arrived.

The point is not credentials. Some of the deepest people in history lacked the certificate. The point is contact: books, tools, materials, feedback, rivals, failed attempts, and the strange humiliating intimacy of knowing exactly how a thing breaks. 4 5 60 62 65

This is where the famous ten-thousand-hours idea becomes useful only after it becomes less magical. Ericsson's deliberate practice was not passive time served. It was effortful work at the edge of current ability with feedback that exposed specific errors. And the meta-analytic correction matters too: practice explains only part of the variance. Depth is necessary, not sufficient. 7 8

The more defensible version comes from John Hayes, who examined roughly five hundred notable compositions by seventy-six eminent composers and found that, with almost no exceptions, no masterwork appeared before about ten years of immersion. Not Mozart's; his early works are competent juvenilia, and the pieces that entered the repertoire came after the decade mark. It is now known, unglamorously, as the ten-year rule. 4

The better metaphor is not a ladder with a prize at the top. It is a set of eyes slowly learning to focus.

At first the beginner sees content. Later they see structure. Later still, they see pressure points: where an assumption is doing work, where a source is too clean, where a trial result is promising but underpowered, where a design will collapse when the second product line arrives.

That final stage is judgment. It is also the stage everyone is most eager to skip.

The economics of the room

Imagine a grant committee at 11:47 p.m. The fluorescent lights are too bright. Coffee has gone cold beside a stack of applications. Every folder contains a plausible problem, a polished method, and a confident paragraph about impact. The reviewers are not choosing between nonsense and genius. They are choosing which of a hundred reasonable ideas deserves a decade of work, a laboratory, a team, and millions of dollars.

At first the room feels rich. Then the arithmetic turns against it. If every existing idea can be combined with every other, the number of possible projects grows faster than any committee can read, test, or fund. The scarce thing is no longer a candidate. It is the capacity to tell which candidate is worth carrying into reality.

How many good ideas can a room evaluate before the room itself becomes the bottleneck?

Martin Weitzman called this recombinant growth: ideas become raw material for more ideas, and the possible combinations multiply far faster than the people who must evaluate them. The implication is easy to miss. An explosion of possible combinations does not automatically create an explosion of progress. It creates an explosion of triage. 19

The binding constraint on innovation is the human and institutional capacity to evaluate, test, and develop an astronomical supply of possible combinations.

There is a small story that makes this abstract point feel physical. Scott Shane gave different entrepreneurs access to the same MIT technology. They did not see the same opportunity waiting inside it. Each person noticed a different use, and the difference was predicted by what they already knew. The technology was identical. The opportunities were not. Prior knowledge had changed the shape of the world they could see. 25

Scientific novelty follows the same pattern. In an analysis of 17.9 million papers, the highest-impact work tended to combine a deep conventional core with a small tail of unusual connections. Novelty helped when it was anchored in enough established knowledge to be understood, tested, and developed. Unfamiliar combinations by themselves produced more variance and lower average usefulness. 20 21

That is why “just generate more ideas” is not an economic strategy. More ideas increase the surface area of search. They also increase the burden on the people who must recognize the rare idea that can survive contact with materials, constraints, competitors, patients, users, or time.

Now zoom out from the committee. Benjamin Jones found that the age at first invention has risen, team size has grown, and specialization has deepened as the stock of knowledge has accumulated. Bloom and colleagues found the macro consequence: ideas are getting harder to find, and research productivity is falling. Progress increasingly requires more people carrying more of the map before anyone can see the next opening. 22 23

The cultural story runs in the opposite direction. We keep looking for the twenty-two-year-old who wins by refusing to learn the old rules. The underlying data is less cinematic. In a Census-based study of 2.7 million founders, the mean age of founders in the fastest-growing top 0.1% was about forty-five, and prior experience in the specific industry was the strongest predictor of success. Youth can bring energy and freedom from convention. It does not repeal the burden of knowledge. 24

The young genius is a memorable exception. Experience is the quiet base rate.

There is an important limit here. Depth is not the same as infallibility. Kahneman and Klein found that expert intuition works best where feedback is rapid, repeated, and unambiguous. Chess and firefighting can install reliable pattern recognition. Long-range markets and political forecasts often cannot. In those domains, the expert may still be the person most capable of framing the question, but their judgment remains a hypothesis that needs testing. 27

That qualification makes the bottleneck worse, not better. If a field has no cheap oracle, it cannot simply appoint an expert and declare the answer solved. It needs people with enough depth to see what might matter, enough independence to challenge one another, and enough institutions to test the few candidates that survive the first cut.

So return to the room. The pile is not valuable because one folder contains an idea. The pile is valuable only if the room can identify that folder before its attention, budget, and patience run out. When machines begin printing ten thousand plausible folders a day, the economics do not make the room less important. They make the room the whole story.

Cut to the machine

Now the door opens and the printer starts.

Every minute, the machine can produce an artifact with the surface of expertise. A grant proposal. A legal memo. A proof sketch. A market analysis. A diagnosis. A molecule. A codebase. A PhD-shaped object with tables, citations, caveats, and the calm tone of someone who knows exactly what they are talking about.

This is not fake progress. It is a real change in the cost of production. The floor rises. More people can make competent-looking work. More first drafts become usable. More bounded tasks get solved. For many purposes, that is wonderful.

But the measured pattern is already visible: AI can improve individual outputs while making them more similar to each other. Doshi and Hauser found that AI assistance raised story ratings, especially for less creative writers, while reducing collective diversity. The floor rose; the room narrowed. 38

The same split appears in research ideation. LLM-generated ideas can be rated highly novel by experts, while struggling on feasibility. That is not a footnote. It is the whole machine in miniature: novelty is cheap; appropriateness remains expensive. 44

The danger is not that the output always fails. The danger is worse: sometimes it fails beautifully.

The labs themselves have documented the problem. Post-training can make models more helpful while damaging calibration, and benchmark incentives can reward confident guessing over saying I do not know. So the object arrives polished, and the alarm that should say check this is unreliable. 47 48

The most expensive thing in the world is a plausible falsehood.

In late 2024, a paper began moving through the AI-and-science conversation with the shape of exactly the result everyone wanted. The title was Artificial Intelligence, Scientific Discovery, and Product Innovation. The author was Aidan Toner-Rodgers, then a PhD student in economics at MIT. The claim was clean enough to travel: an AI tool had been rolled out inside a large materials-science lab, and the researchers with access to it supposedly discovered 44% more materials, filed 39% more patents, and produced 17% more product prototypes. 51

The story was almost engineered to be irresistible. It was not another demo. It was not a chatbot writing a poem. It looked like field evidence from a real industrial lab: more than a thousand scientists, a staggered deployment, innovation measured in materials and patents. It seemed to answer the question everyone was asking in 2024: does AI merely make knowledge work feel faster, or does it actually accelerate discovery?

And the answer appeared to be yes. The paper spread before peer review had finished. Major outlets covered the finding. It was discussed by economists, technologists, investors, and policy people as one of the first clean empirical signals that AI could speed up science itself. David Autor and Daron Acemoglu, two of MIT's most prominent economists, later said the paper had become known and discussed in the literature on AI and science even though it had not appeared in a refereed journal.

That is what made it dangerous. It did not look like spam. It did not look like a hallucinated citation at the bottom of a student essay. It looked like a rare piece of evidence from inside the machinery of scientific production. It had numbers. It had institutional proximity. It had the right topic at the right moment. It looked like the kind of thing people could build forecasts, funding arguments, and public stories around.

Then the floor gave way.

In May 2025, MIT said that, after concerns were raised and an internal confidential review was conducted, it had no confidence in the provenance, reliability, or validity of the data and no confidence in the veracity of the research. MIT asked arXiv and the Quarterly Journal of Economics to withdraw or mark the paper accordingly, and said the author was no longer at MIT. Acemoglu and Autor publicly stated that the findings should not be relied on in academic or public discussions. 51

Notice the shape of the failure. The problem was not that the paper looked sloppy to everyone. The problem was that it looked good enough to move through elite filters before the right kind of scrutiny caught up. According to reporting, a computer scientist with materials-science experience raised concerns that helped bring the issue to MIT. That is the whole essay in miniature: the artifact had the surface of expertise, but the error became visible only to someone with enough domain contact to ask whether the described tool, lab, data, and workflow made sense.

A plausible falsehood is expensive because it borrows the trust reserved for truth.

AI does not remove the verification problem. It multiplies the number of things that need verification.

The verifier map

Start where the machine really did look like magic: a Go board in Seoul.

In March 2016, AlphaGo played Lee Sedol, one of the greatest Go players alive, and won four games to one. The famous move came in Game 2: move 37, a stone placed so strangely that professional commentators first treated it as a mistake. Much later in the game, the board revealed the answer. The move had been waiting for the future. 71

That moment matters because it was genuinely astonishing. It was not a fake demo. It was not a marketing page. It was a public contest inside a world with rules sharp enough to cut glass. A Go move is legal or illegal. A game is won or lost. A self-play system can play again, and again, and again, receiving a hard answer every time.

Why could AI become superhuman there?

Because the board was a machine for judgment. The model could generate moves, but the game could punish them. Search had somewhere to go. Reinforcement learning had a gradient. Millions of trials were not millions of opinions; they were millions of encounters with a rule-bound world that returned a result.

The tighter the verifier, the more generation can turn into progress.

Now leave the board and watch the magic thin out.

Protein structure prediction is another real achievement. AlphaFold mattered because the field had benchmarks, physical constraints, and experimentally determined structures to compare against. But a predicted structure is not a medicine. A binding hypothesis is not efficacy. A promising molecule is not a safe drug. Between a protein model and a treatment on the market sit wet labs, synthesis, assays, toxicity, dosing, patients, regulators, failures, and time. The next section follows that chain in detail. 55 30 52

Code has the same split. A function with tests has a usable external check. The compiler, type checker, and test suite can say yes or no, and the machine can iterate quickly. But Figma, Ubuntu, or Stripe are not functions. They are long-lived systems with invisible obligations: latency, architecture, correctness under extension, security, permissions, drivers, package dependencies, money movement, collaboration, backwards compatibility, user trust. The demo can pass while the product is structurally wrong.

This is why productivity is hard to measure even for experts. In METR's early-2025 study, experienced open-source developers expected AI to speed them up and reported afterwards that it had; measured time showed a slowdown. METR later noted that newer tools and adoption patterns complicate the picture. The stable lesson is calibration: even experts can misread whether AI helped when the verifier is not a simple pass/fail signal. 40 41

Writing has the same trap in softer form. A paragraph can be locally fluent. A long essay has to hold a thesis across distance. A great novel has to sustain voice, character, plot, memory, surprise, moral pressure, and reader trust for hundreds of pages. There is no compiler for Anna Karenina. There is no unit test for whether a chapter earns the next one.

Mathematics, again, has to be handled carefully. Formal systems can check fully formalized arguments. Ordinary mathematical prose is not automatically formal. A proof assistant can help once the work has been translated into its language, but the choice of theorem, the formalization, the connection to the intended claim, and the community judgment are still human work.

The AI-related Erdős-problem examples are therefore case studies, not a base-rate proof that mathematics has become easy to automate or verify. They show a useful division of labor: the machine can search; mathematicians still frame, recognize, repair, and certify. 49

So yes, AI can help the process. Absolutely. It can generate candidates, compress first drafts, search a design space, propose tests, write scaffolding, find analogies, and make competent work cheaper. But creating an artifact that matters in the world is different from creating an artifact that looks complete on a screen. The world does not give one exam. It gives many, and most of them are slow.

Spending two million AI agents on a problem does not change this if the agents do not have a hard way to know which outputs are real. More agents can mean more search. It can also mean more polished candidates for humans to evaluate. Without a verifier, scale is not judgment. It is volume.

The frontier labs reveal this with their hiring. OpenAI advertises roles asking for 7+ years of software engineering experience on research-platform and frontier-systems work. Google DeepMind lists senior machine-learning engineering roles with multi-year engineering and AI experience. Anthropic advertises Senior+ and Staff-level infrastructure and research-tooling roles. If frontier models had made deep engineering judgment cheap, the labs closest to the technology would be hiring armies of clever beginners and letting the models self-improve around them. Instead they are paying for experienced people who can build, evaluate, debug, and hold complex systems together. 72

This is the jagged frontier problem. Inside the frontier, AI assistance can improve performance. Outside it, people accept plausible output and get worse. The catch is that you need domain knowledge to see where the frontier is. 39

Domain Verifier Prediction
Go Legal moves, self-play, win/loss Superhuman play becomes possible
Small coding tasks Compiler, tests, runtime Fast local gains
Product-grade software Architecture, security, scale, users, time Expert guidance remains load-bearing
Protein structure Benchmarks and experimental comparison Real progress, bounded artifact
Drug discovery Wet lab, clinical trials, regulators Many costly reality checks
Long fiction or serious essays Reader memory, taste, culture, time No cheap pass/fail signal
Mathematical claims Expert proof review; formal checks when formalized Search can help, certification is not automatic

The pattern is simple enough to be uncomfortable. The easier it is to grade the work, the more powerful generation becomes. The harder and more layered the reality checks become, the more valuable human judgment becomes.

The cancer drug

Now take the story people most want to be true.

A teenager sits in a bedroom, prompts a model, and finds a cure for cancer.

I understand why that story is irresistible. It is generous. It lets intelligence outrun institutions. It turns medicine into a locked door that only needed the right sentence. It gives the hero a laptop instead of a billion-dollar lab.

But the first generated molecule is not the end of the movie. It is the title card.

Target identification has to survive the literature, and parts of the biomedical literature do not survive contact with replication. Biology has tacit knowledge, bad maps, cell lines that lie, assays that drift, mouse models that flatter, and human bodies that refuse to behave like diagrams.

This is why written knowledge has two ceilings: some of what matters is tacit and travels through practice, and some of what is written is simply wrong. Collins's TEA-laser work and the preclinical reproducibility cases are not side stories. They are warnings about any system trained primarily on the record. 31 32 33 34

After the molecule comes synthesis. Then assays. Then pharmacokinetics. Then toxicity. Then an IND filing. Then Phase I, II, and III. Then regulators. Then doctors. Then patients whose lives cannot be debugged after deployment.

Oncology is the harshest version of this verifier. Across large clinical-trial datasets, oncology has extremely low Phase I-to-approval success and a long median time in the clinic. Reality returns its answer slowly and at enormous cost. 30

As of July 20, 2026, the strongest AI-originated drug-discovery story is still not a pharmacy-shelf story. Insilico's rentosertib has entered Phase III for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, a major milestone, and the company's own release says the drug remains investigational and has not been approved by any regulatory authority. 52

That is the real movie: AI helped originate a target and molecule, and then a company full of scientists, laboratories, trials, capital, regulators, and patients had to carry the claim toward reality.

In medicine, the verifier is not a vibes check. It is a decade of biology asking whether you were serious.

The false shortcut

At this point, the obvious objection walks in wearing a lab coat.

Fine. Use AI to check AI.

Run three models. Make them debate. Build a panel. Have one generate, one criticize, one judge. It sounds like peer review, and it is exactly the kind of thing that works in demos because the transcript looks like deliberation.

The problem is that peer review is not several voices. It is a social machine with adversarial incentives, independent histories, public standards, reputational cost, and access to reality.

Condorcet-style group advantage depends on independence. Longino's account of objectivity depends on actual communities that can criticize and take up criticism. Popper's picture of knowledge depends on attempted refutation. A set of similar models trained on overlapping corpora and post-trained toward agreeable helpfulness is not that social machine. 35 36 37 46

The car-wash question makes the problem comically small. If the car wash is 100 meters away, should you walk or drive? Many models were pulled toward walk by the distance cue, missing the goal constraint: the car has to be at the car wash. The model can possess the fact and fail to apply the constraint. 50

Scale that up. A model can possess sentences about clinical trial design and still miss the constraint that makes the proposed trial useless. It can possess sentences about distributed systems and still miss the abstraction that will fail under load. It can possess sentences about proof technique and still let one hidden assumption carry the bridge.

Banerjee offers a useful architectural version of the same limit: a model can interpolate within the captured horizon of its training experience, but recombining that record does not by itself supply an unobserved, long-horizon constraint. When a proposal depends on such a constraint, verification still requires an expert community with access to the relevant reality. 73

Three models sharing the same blind spot do not become three independent judges. They become one blind spot with better formatting.

And the deeper loop is worse: models can prefer their own style of answer, and models trained recursively on generated output can degrade. A verification system cannot live forever on its own exhaust. It needs contact with independent human and physical ground truth. 45 54

The apprenticeship trap

Now the story turns, because the danger is not only that AI needs experts.

The danger is that AI may reduce the number of people becoming experts.

A junior analyst used to read the ugly source documents. A junior scientist used to fight the assay. A junior engineer used to trace the bug through the codebase. A student used to sit with the blank page long enough to discover which part they did not understand.

Those tasks are annoying, slow, and often beneath the glamour of the field. They are also how judgment is installed.

Bainbridge named the pattern in 1983: automation often leaves the human responsible for the exceptional case while removing the routine practice that would keep the human capable of handling it. The person is retained as backup and trained into helplessness at the same time. 43

Modern knowledge work shows the same shape. In survey work from Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon, higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical thinking, while higher confidence in one's own ability was associated with more. Work shifts from doing to supervising. But supervision is not an entry-level skill. 42

This is the loop that should make everyone nervous:

We need experts to verify machine output.

Experts are made by doing the hard parts.

The machine is increasingly used to remove the hard parts.

The system consumes its own precondition.

The labs reveal this with their own budgets. Expert-evaluation markets pay far more for physicians, lawyers, PhDs, executives, and domain specialists than for general annotation, because the frontier model still needs scarce human judgment as ground truth. Generation got cheap. Verification did not. 53

That price signal is not reassurance. It is a warning flare. The market is bidding up the very thing the culture keeps telling young people they no longer need to become.

Back to P = NP

So return to the opening scene, after the first week of claims.

The LinkedIn post is still there. The Reddit PDF is still being argued over. The GitHub repository has more stars now, and a new issue says the claimed Lean formalization does not prove the theorem stated in the README. The website has been translated. The YouTube series has spawned response videos. None of this is yet the conventional announcement people imagined. It is a public queue of independent answer-shaped objects.

What would it take for one of them to become knowledge?

Not attention. Attention is easy now. Not confidence. Confidence can be generated. Not length, diagrams, citations, model names, or a calm explanation voice. The claim has to pass from the cheap side of the verifier map to the expensive side, where people can say exactly what has been proved and why the hard step has not merely been renamed.

First, the field would need to identify the claim. Is it a proof of P = NP, a proof of P not equal to NP, a conditional result, a misunderstanding of the problem, or a clever reduction that hides the hard step inside a definition?

Second, experts would need to read it. Not skim it. Read it with the full violence of professional attention: test the definitions, isolate the load-bearing lemmas, check the reductions, look for smuggled assumptions, try to break it, try to formalize it, try to explain it to one another.

Third, the proof would need a community. Independent readers. Different priors. People with incentives to find the flaw. Formal tools where they genuinely apply. Public standards. Time.

A formal checker could be part of that story, but there is no button that compiles ordinary mathematical prose into truth. Someone has to choose what to formalize, understand what the formalization means, connect it to the domain, and decide whether the proof has actually answered the question people care about. 49

This is why P = NP is not like AlphaGo. The board can punish a bad move immediately. A compiler can reject a broken program immediately. A clinical trial can reject a drug slowly, brutally, and expensively. But a major proof claim lives in a stranger space: there is a right answer, yet the path to recognizing it runs through trained human judgment, formalization where possible, and a community strong enough to survive a flood of plausible falsehoods. 30 71

That is why the question in the title is not a joke. We might know because a field still exists that can know. Or we might not know quickly, because the answer arrives inside a pile so large that the field's reading capacity becomes the bottleneck.

Where there is no cheap external verifier, the verifier is a trained human community.

And that community is not a decoration around the breakthrough. It is the instrument by which a breakthrough becomes knowledge.

Predictions

An argument this strong should make bets. Here are mine.

  1. The first approved AI-originated drug will come from an organization dense with domain experts, physical laboratories, clinical infrastructure, and regulatory skill.
  2. AI progress will remain fastest where outputs meet usable external feedback: tests, compilers, simulations, clear scoring functions, and formal systems when the relevant work has actually been formalized.
  3. In domains without usable feedback loops, AI will produce more plausible candidates than institutions can responsibly evaluate.
  4. Mean output quality will rise while collective diversity falls, especially in writing, design, pitch decks, code scaffolds, and research ideation.
  5. The market price of verification skill will rise even while public respect for expertise becomes more fragile.
  6. Model ensembles will help on bounded tasks but will show diminishing returns when the failure mode is shared constraint blindness.
  7. If AI truly substitutes for accumulated expertise, the age and experience burden of top innovators should fall. If it does not fall, the expertise bottleneck is still binding.

The final prediction is the one I care about most: societies that protect apprenticeship will outperform societies that only buy tools.

Because the pile is coming either way. The future belongs to whoever can read it.
